# A Five-Minute Stock-Market Workout, Graded by the Market Itself

*Munymo turns the daily-puzzle formula into a head-to-head stock duel. The loop is clever and the design is polished. The scoreboard, for now, is mostly robots.*

**By a Personal Technology critic, The Wall Street Journal** — *written by Claude Fable 5.1 at the founder's request, 2026-09-17, as an outside-view exercise*

---

Every weekday evening, a little after the closing bell, a website called Munymo posts a fight card. On Wednesday it was Bank of America against Goldman Sachs. The day before, Lennar took on D.R. Horton. Your job is to say which of the two stocks will have the better day, and then to wait for the market to tell you whether you were right.

That is the whole game. It takes about five minutes, it costs nothing, there is no money on the line, and you can only play once a day. If that sounds like Wordle with tickers, the site's own FAQ agrees with you, and the comparison is fair. Munymo has borrowed the daily-puzzle formula wholesale and bolted it onto something more interesting than a five-letter word: a real, unresolved question about two real companies, settled by real prices.

## How it plays

The sequence is the smartest thing about it. You are asked to pick first on instinct alone, before you have seen a single number. Only then does the site show you the research: a plain-English brief on why these two companies are paired today, a table of sixteen metrics with a "what does this mean?" button on every one of them, a chart for each stock, and a link to the news article that prompted the matchup. You declare that you have read it, and only then do you make the pick that counts.

Then comes a timed question about what you just read. It is worth 20 points of a possible 100; the prediction is worth 80. Answer within fifteen seconds and you get all 20. Dawdle for a minute and you get 12. Get it wrong and you get nothing. The point, the site says, is to reward reading carefully. In practice it also stops you from clicking through the research with your eyes shut, which is the failure mode of every "educational" game ever built.

Picks lock at 9:30 a.m. New York time, when the market opens. If you made a gut pick and forgot to come back, the site submits it for you, which keeps your streak alive and is exactly the kind of small mercy most apps forget. Results arrive after the close by email and push notification, with a debrief that is better than it needs to be: the two closing moves, how the crowd voted, a "what happened" summary, a longer "Hindsight Spotlight" essay on why the day went the way it did, and a three-minute lesson tied to something in the matchup.

There is a 32-lesson curriculum, free, that runs from "what a share actually is" to reading analyst positioning. There is a practice mode that lets you replay any past matchup, end to end, with the date and result hidden. There is a leaderboard you can only join after ten live games, with a paragraph explaining, correctly, that an average over two games is luck. The design is clean, fast, readable on a phone, and free of the dark patterns that infest the fantasy-sports apps this superficially resembles. Nothing is for sale. Nothing is a recommendation. The disclaimer says, in so many words, please do not bet your retirement on a daily prediction game.

## The catch, and there is more than one

The first problem is philosophical and the site half-admits it. Guessing which of two large-cap stocks will have the better single day is, over any honest sample, close to a coin flip. The founder's answer is that the outcome is not really the point; the daily repetition and the incidental learning are. That is a reasonable position for a training app. It is a shaky one for a leaderboard, and Munymo has a leaderboard.

The second problem is that leaderboard. When I looked this week, six players had qualified. One was the founder. The other five were named Claire B, Adam A, Daniel D, Big B and Erica E, each with exactly 44 games played, and each is a test account the site runs to exercise its own pipeline. The same five bots make up most of the "crowd" whose voting split is shown after every result. A newcomer sees a community. It is a mannequin.

The third problem is the fine print on the fight itself. Munymo's rules say the winner is the stock with the better percentage move from the open to the close. The result cards print both the open and closing prices and the percentage, and on roughly a third of the games I checked, the arithmetic does not agree with the percentage on the card. On July 6, Broadcom was declared the winner over Nvidia at +3.55% against +0.37%. Do the division on the prices printed underneath and Nvidia moved more. The same reversal appears twice more in the archive. The automated system that settles each day's game appears to sometimes use the day's change against the prior close, and sometimes the open-to-close figure. For a product whose central promise is "the market grades you," the grading has to be exact.

Fourth: the research. The site's homepage calls it "an expert-curated research brief." It is written each afternoon by an artificial-intelligence agent with a web search tool, and published without a human reading it first. The briefs I read were clear, well-sourced and, as far as I could check, accurate. Machine authorship is not the problem. Calling it expert curation without saying so is.

## What is coming, allegedly

Munymo is in beta and says so on every page. Much of what it advertises is still on the road ahead: a composite "MunyIQ" score with gemstone-tier cards from Sapphire to Diamond, verifiable certificates for your résumé, private head-to-head leagues, native apps. Signing up now records you as a "founding member" who will be recognized when those things arrive. None of this is hidden; all of it is labelled "coming soon." It is still a lot of the pitch.

What the site does not have yet is the one thing that made its model famous. Wordle spread because every result was a shareable little grid of colored squares. Munymo has no share button at all. It also never shows you the answer to its own best question: it asks you to pick twice, on instinct and then after research, precisely so you can learn whether research helps you. After forty games there is still no screen that adds it up.

## The verdict

Munymo is a better idea than most fintech gamification, executed with more restraint than most of it. The daily loop is tight, the research screen is genuinely educational, the debrief teaches, and the practice mode and lessons mean you are never stuck waiting for tomorrow. If a friend who was curious about markets and afraid of them asked me for something to do every morning with a coffee, I would send them here before I sent them to a brokerage's paper-trading account.

I would also tell them to ignore the leaderboard until it has people on it, to check the arithmetic on the result cards, and to remember that the "expert" writing the brief does not sleep. Munymo's founder has said the point of the beta is to let real people pick it apart. Consider this a start.
